It is half past ten and the deck should have been finished two hours ago. I am
dragging a text box three pixels to the left so it lines up with the one on the
previous slide, and I am doing it by eye, because there is nothing in the file
that knows those two boxes are the same box. I have been making presentations
for twenty years. This is still the job. Why, exactly, is this still drawing?

## A slide is a drawing, and that is the problem

Open any deck in any of the big three applications and look at what the file
actually contains. A slide is a rectangle with things floating on it: a text
box here, an image there, each one holding a position and a size and a font.
That is all. The file does not know that the text box at the top is a title. It
does not know that the five lines below it are a list. It does not know that
the white text you just dropped is sitting on the bright part of a photograph.

The alternative has a boring name and an obvious shape. A slide is a record
with fields: a type, a title, a list of items, a source. Not a picture of a
list, but a list. Not a drawing that happens to look like a timeline, but a
timeline that knows its own dates.

Everything that is wrong with presentations follows from which of those two
things your tool actually stores.

## What goes wrong

The first cost is time. Every deck starts at zero and is built by hand, and
because there is nothing to program against, none of it can be automated. You
cannot ask a canvas for its third bullet. There is no third bullet; there are
coordinates.

The second cost is quality, and let us be honest about it: most decks are bad.
Walls of text, because nothing knows how many lines fit. A house style
reinvented per slide, because the house style is a habit rather than a rule.
Bullets that all appear at once, because the setting that reveals them one by
one is buried three dialogs deep and almost nobody has ever found it. White
titles laid over the bright half of a photograph, because no tool in this
category has ever once measured that.

The third cost arrives afterwards. A drawing has no semantics, so there is
nothing to hand a screen reader. A deck is a file rather than a URL, so the web
gets a download link at best. And the whole thing sits in a closed format in
somebody else's cloud, which is a different post entirely: I wrote it as
[Where your slides actually live](/blog/where-your-slides-actually-live/).

The web-first tools of the last few years do parts of this properly, and some
of them do it beautifully. But it is always a piece of the puzzle, and the
format still belongs to them.

## Turn it around

Give the slide a type and every complaint above stops being a character trait
and becomes a solvable problem. The type knows how many items it can hold, so
the wall of text can be caught before it is a wall. The house style is a render
rule instead of a per-slide decision. Revealing items one at a time is
behaviour of the type, not a checkbox in a dialog. Contrast is measurable, so
it can be guaranteed. Semantics exist, so accessibility and the web are not
extra work. And there is finally something to program against.

There is a real trade in here and it should be said out loud: you give up the
canvas. You cannot put that box exactly there, at that angle, because there is
no there. What you get back is a deck that is consistent without anyone
policing it, readable without anyone checking it, and automatable without
anyone screen-scraping it. If your work genuinely needs a free canvas, use a
canvas. Most decks are not that; most decks are a title and some items,
redrawn by hand for the four hundredth time.

## What that looks like in practice

I use this every day, which is the only reason I trust the argument. A typical
evening now: "insert a slide after slide 12 with the milestones from that Notion
database." It fetches the data and adds a timeline slide. Or: "make a slide with
the faces of the advisory board", and because the ImageKit media library is
connected, that arrives complete in one go.

Yes, that is AI. But it works because of the structure, not because of the
model. The model is not drawing a picture of a timeline; it is filling in a
record that gets validated before it is allowed on the slide. That is a much
smaller and much more reliable job.

And if you want nothing to do with AI, the same door is open: it is an API, and
you can write the script yourself. The assistant is a client here, not the
foundation.

## So: open source, and a spec

Two decisions follow from all of this, and only one of them is obvious.

Open source is the obvious one. You can read every line, run the whole thing on
your own server, and change what does not suit you, without asking anyone.

The [spec](/spec/) is the one that matters more. An application can be
abandoned; a documented format with published schemas can be implemented by
somebody else. That is the actual promise against lock-in, and it is the reason
the deck format has its own section on this site rather than a paragraph in the
documentation.

I am aware of the size of that ambition. This is an attempt to put a standard
under something that three of the largest companies in the world already have a
format for, and whether it works is genuinely an open question. Fine. I am
putting a stake in the ground, because I use this every day and it is simply
better.

## Am I the only one who sees it this way

That is the honest question at the end of this. I think the canvas is the
reason presentations are the way they are, and I would like to know whether the
problem I feel is a problem you recognise.

If it is: trying it is one line in a terminal. The repository has exactly one
star, and it is mine, so there is room for improvement there too. And if you
have built something in this direction, or think this is wrong, tell me. I
would rather hear it than not.

Let's build some cool shit.
