Four generated decks have landed in my inbox this month. Every one of them had
a slide with eleven bullet points on it. Every one of them put white text on the
bright half of a stock photograph. Every one of them was, in the strict
technical sense, a sequence of pictures of sentences. And not one of them could
have been rescued by a better prompt.

The easy conclusion is that the AI is bad at making slides. I think that is
wrong, and the real answer is more annoying: it did exactly what we asked. We
pointed it at a file format that cannot hold anything except a drawing, and it
drew.

## The generate button is aimed at the wrong thing

Open any deck in any of the big three and look at what is actually in the file.
A slide is a rectangle with things floating on it. A text box here, an image
there, each one carrying a position, a size and a font, and that is the entire
contents. The file does not know that the box at the top is a title. It does not
know that the five lines underneath are a list. It certainly does not know that
the white text you just dropped is sitting on the one part of the photograph
that is also white.

So when a model generates a deck, there is nothing for it to fill in. There is
only a canvas to cover. Nothing can check the result, because there is no claim
to check: no field that says "this is a title and titles are one line", no rule
that says "this type takes at most five items", no way to ask whether the text
is legible against what is behind it. The output is unreviewable by
construction. We have automated the production of things nobody can inspect,
which is a strange thing to be excited about.

## It was already a mess. AI industrialised it.

Let us be honest about the starting point, because "AI made presentations worse"
is only half a sentence. Presentations were already bad.

Walls of text, because nothing anywhere knows how many lines fit on a slide. A
house style reinvented from scratch on every slide, because the house style is a
habit rather than a rule the file can enforce. Bullets that all arrive at once,
because the setting that reveals them one at a time has been buried three
dialogs deep for thirty years and approximately nobody has found it. Titles laid
over the bright half of a photograph, because no tool in this category has ever
measured that, not once, not for anybody.

And then the part nobody even complains about, because it has always been this
way: a deck is inaccessible by construction. A drawing has no semantics, so
there is nothing to hand a screen reader. A deck is a file rather than a URL, so
the web gets a download link if it gets anything. This is the medium
organisations use to say what they think, and it ships as a picture book that
half the audience cannot read.

None of that is because the people making decks are lazy. It is because the tool
knows nothing, and a tool that knows nothing cannot stop anything. Point a
generator at it and you do not get better slides. You get the same slides, much
faster, in industrial quantities.

The web-first tools of the last few years do parts of this properly, and some of
them do it beautifully. But it is always one piece of the puzzle, and the format
underneath still belongs to them.

## Slides should be data, not drawings

Here is the whole idea, and it is not complicated. A slide is a record with
fields: a type, a title, a list of items, a source. Not a picture of a list, but
a list. Not a drawing that happens to resemble a timeline, but a timeline that
knows its own dates.

Give a slide a type and every complaint above stops being a personality trait
and becomes a solvable problem. The type knows how many items it holds, so the
wall of text can be refused before it is a wall. The house style is a render
rule rather than a decision taken forty times per deck. Revealing items one at a
time is behaviour of the type, not a checkbox somebody has to find. Contrast is
measurable, so it can be guaranteed instead of hoped for. Semantics exist, so
accessibility and the web stop being extra work. And there is finally something
to program against.

There is a real trade here and it should be said out loud: you give up the
canvas. You cannot put that box exactly there, at that angle, because there is
no there. What you get instead is a deck that is consistent without anyone
policing it, legible without anyone checking it, and automatable without anyone
scraping it off a screen. If your work genuinely needs a free canvas, use a
canvas, and enjoy it. Most decks are not that. Most decks are a title and some
items, hand-drawn for the four hundredth time by somebody who would rather be
doing something else.

## Now put the AI back in

This is the part that convinced me, and it is why I am not writing an
anti-AI post. I use this every day.

A normal evening now: "insert a slide after slide 12 with the milestones from
that Notion database." It fetches the data and adds a timeline slide. Or: "make
a slide with the faces of the advisory board", and because the media library is
connected, that arrives complete, in one go, correctly cropped, with the names
under the right faces.

That works because of the structure, not because of the model. The model is not
drawing a picture of a timeline. It is filling in a record, which is then
validated before it is allowed onto a slide. That is a much smaller job and a
far more reliable one, and it is the difference between an assistant that helps
and a slop machine that produces eleven bullet points.

And if you want nothing to do with AI, the same door is open and it is the same
door. It is an API. Write the script yourself, on a cron, from your own systems.
The assistant is a client here, not the foundation. That distinction matters,
because a foundation you cannot replace is just a landlord.

## While we are at it, it should be yours

There is a second thing that falls out of this, and it is the one I actually
care about on the days when the typography does not keep me up.

Presentations are where organisations say what they think, out loud, on the
record. That material currently lives on infrastructure nobody in the building
has seen, in a format nobody in the building can open without the vendor, under
terms nobody in the building negotiated. We have all quietly agreed that this is
fine. It is not fine, and the good news is that it stopped being necessary. You
can wire your own things together now. Your own server, your own media library,
your own database, your own model if you want one. I go further into that in
[Where your slides actually live](/blog/where-your-slides-actually-live/).

## The size of the ambition, since we are being honest

Two decisions follow, and only one of them is obvious.

Open source is the obvious one. Read every line, run it on your own server,
change what does not suit you, ask nobody.

The [spec](/spec/) is the one that matters more. An application can be
abandoned. A documented format with published schemas can be picked up and
implemented by somebody else. That is the actual promise against lock-in, and it
is why the deck format has its own section on this site instead of a paragraph
in the manual.

I am aware of how that sounds. This is an attempt to put a standard underneath
something that three of the largest companies on earth already have a format
for, from a project with, at the time of writing, exactly one GitHub star, which
is mine. Whether it works is a genuinely open question.

Fine. I am putting a stake in the ground anyway, because I use this every day
and it is simply better, and because the alternative is another decade of
industrially generated picture books that nobody can read and nobody can check.
